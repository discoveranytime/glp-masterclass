💊 The popular GLP medications masterclass

Twenty-two compounds, one hormone playbook

These medicines all work on hormones that control hunger, blood sugar, and how the body uses energy. Most are called GLP-1 drugs — but some hit extra hormone pathways. They're given as shots (usually weekly), used mainly for type 2 diabetes or weight loss — and several are still in trials or only available in certain countries.

⚡ Quick Read

  • Same playbook: gut-hormone signals that quiet hunger, slow the stomach, and steady blood sugar — with newer drugs stacking extra pathways (GIP, glucagon, amylin).
  • The range is wide: trial weight loss runs from ~3–6% (dulaglutide) to ~24–28% (retatrutide) — different trials, different people, not a ranking.
  • Status varies: eight are FDA-approved — the newest, orforglipron, is a once-daily pill; mazdutide and beinaglutide are approved in China; albiglutide was withdrawn (commercial, not safety); the rest are investigational, early-stage, or halted.
  • Side effects rhyme: nausea and gut issues lead everywhere; slow dose increases keep them manageable.
  • Combos are the frontier: cagrilintide + semaglutide (CagriSema) shows ~20–22% — more than either alone.
  • Twenty years of stacking: from twice-daily exenatide (2005) to a GLP-1 pill (2026) and monthly shots in trials — more pathways, longer intervals, easier formats.
01

How they all work

One playbook, five hormone levers.

Nearly every drug here copies or amplifies a gut hormone your body already makes:

🟢GLP-1 — the headline hormone. Quiets hunger in the brain, slows how fast food leaves the stomach, and helps the body handle sugar. Every drug on this list touches GLP-1 — except cagrilintide.
🟡GIP — a second gut hormone. Boosts insulin, may calm the gut side effects of GLP-1, and adds its own appetite effect. Tirzepatide and retatrutide use it.
🔥Glucagon — usually raises blood sugar, but in these drugs it's dosed to burn more energy and melt liver fat. Retatrutide, mazdutide, and survodutide use it.
🟣Amylin — a satiety hormone released with insulin after meals. Cagrilintide copies it; it works in different brain regions than GLP-1, which is why the combo hits harder.
The pattern: each new generation stacks another hormone onto GLP-1 — and average weight loss has climbed with each stack. That's the whole story of this drug class in one sentence.
02

The receptor map

Where each of the nine sits on the hormone map.

Think of it as five strategies, from simplest to most stacked:

  • GLP-1 alone: exenatide, liraglutide, lixisenatide, albiglutide (withdrawn), dulaglutide, beinaglutide, semaglutide — the proven foundation
  • Oral small-molecule GLP-1: orforglipron — a daily pill, no peptide, no fasting rules
  • GLP-1 + GIP: tirzepatide, ribupatide — the double act
  • GLP-1 + glucagon: mazdutide, survodutide, cotadutide (halted), efinopegdutide, pemvidutide — the energy-burners
  • GLP-1 + GIP + glucagon: retatrutide — the triple, highest trial numbers so far
  • Amylin: cagrilintide, petrelintide — the satiety partners that pair with GLP-1
  • GLP-1 + amylin in one molecule: amycretin — oral and injectable versions
  • GLP-1 + GIP blockade: maritide — monthly; blocks GIP instead of activating it
  • Multi-agonist (early): bioglutide — investigational, described as hitting up to four pathways including IGF-1

3

Exenatide

Apr 2005

The first GLP-1 — twice-daily pioneer.

Exenatide (Byetta / Bydureon)FDA-approved

The first GLP-1 ever approved (2005) — a synthetic copy of exendin-4, a hormone first found in Gila monster saliva. Byetta is taken twice a day; the weekly version Bydureon followed in 2012.

Maker: Amylin / AstraZenecaReceptors: GLP-1Form: twice-daily shot (weekly version too)
2–3 kg typical weight loss — modest by today's standards

The proof of concept the entire class was built on — modest on the scale, historic in significance.

4

Liraglutide

Jan 2010

The daily original — first, not strongest.

Liraglutide (Saxenda / Victoza)FDA-approved

An older daily GLP-1 shot — Saxenda for weight loss, Victoza for diabetes. Same mechanism as the weeklies, just with a shot every day.

Maker: Novo NordiskReceptors: GLP-1Form: daily pen
6–8% weight loss (Saxenda)

Well-tested with a long safety record and sometimes better insurance coverage — but the daily routine and modest numbers have it losing ground to the weeklies.

5

Lixisenatide

Feb 2013

The daily short-actor.

Lixisenatide (Adlyxin / Lyxumia)FDA-approved

A short-acting daily GLP-1 (EMA 2013, FDA 2016 as Adlyxin) with its strongest effect on mealtime blood sugar. The ELIXA trial confirmed heart safety — but not heart benefit.

Maker: SanofiReceptors: GLP-1Form: daily shot
2–3 kg typical weight loss

A diabetes-focused daily that the weeklies largely eclipsed.

6

Albiglutide

Apr 2014 – Jul 2017

The one that left the market.

Albiglutide (Tanzeum)Withdrawn

A weekly GLP-1 fused to albumin for long action (FDA 2014, Tanzeum). It worked — and was withdrawn in 2017–18 for commercial reasons, not safety.

Maker: GSKReceptors: GLP-1Form: weekly shot (was)
— A1c-focused; modest weight loss

The reminder that "withdrawn" doesn't always mean "dangerous." Sometimes the market just moves on.

7

Dulaglutide

Sep 2014

The steady diabetes workhorse with heart data.

Dulaglutide (Trulicity)FDA-approved

A weekly GLP-1 shot mainly for type 2 diabetes — lowers blood sugar, with modest weight loss on the side. Also approved to lower heart risks in some people with diabetes (REWIND trial).

Maker: Eli LillyReceptors: GLP-1Form: weekly pen
3–6% weight loss, higher at top doses

The pick when the main job is blood sugar with modest weight help — long track record, strong heart-outcome data, less dramatic on the scale.

8

Beinaglutide

2016

China's mealtime GLP-1.

BeinaglutideApproved in China

China's mealtime GLP-1 (NMPA-approved 2016) — a recombinant human GLP-1 identical to the natural hormone, injected with each meal rather than weekly.

Maker: Benemae Bio (China)Receptors: GLP-1Form: 2–3× daily with meals
~6% weight loss at 16 weeks (Chinese adults, overweight/obesity)
— has a weight-management indication in China

Old-school rhythm, still prescribed in China — including for weight management, with mostly mild stomach effects in studies.

9

Semaglutide

Dec 2017

The household name — proven, weekly, with heart data.

Semaglutide (Ozempic / Wegovy)FDA-approved

The best-known GLP-1 — copies the gut hormone to keep you full longer, slow the stomach, and steady blood sugar. The weekly shot that made the class a household name; its oral sibling gets the next chapter.

Maker: Novo NordiskReceptors: GLP-1Form: weekly shot
12–15% weight loss (Wegovy, ~68 weeks)

Strong evidence, weekly dosing, proven heart benefits in some groups — its main disadvantage versus tirzepatide is slightly lower average weight loss.

10

Rybelsus

Sep 2019

The pill that proved a GLP-1 could survive the stomach.

Rybelsus (oral semaglutide)FDA-approved

Same molecule as Ozempic and Wegovy — semaglutide — in a daily tablet. The trick is SNAC, a carrier that shields the peptide from stomach acid just long enough to be absorbed. The first oral GLP-1, FDA-approved September 2019 for type 2 diabetes.

Maker: Novo NordiskReceptors: GLP-1Form: daily pill
14% fewer major heart events (SOUL, 9,650 people)

The trade-off is ritual: empty stomach, a small sip of water, then 30 minutes before any food or drink — or absorption collapses. PIONEER-1 showed A1c down 1.4% at 26 weeks (14 mg); weight loss is modest, since this is a diabetes drug first. A 25 mg obesity version (Wegovy pill) followed in December 2025.

11

Tirzepatide

May 2022

The double act that reset expectations.

Tirzepatide (Mounjaro / Zepbound)FDA-approved

Hits two hormones at once: GLP-1 and GIP. The double action consistently beats semaglutide alone on the scale — confirmed in head-to-head testing — and Zepbound also carries a sleep apnea indication.

Maker: Eli LillyReceptors: GLP-1 + GIPForm: weekly shot
15–22% weight loss over ~72 weeks

The current approved heavyweight — bigger average losses and better blood-sugar results than the GLP-1 singles, at the cost of stronger early side effects and higher prices.

12

Cotadutide

halted · Apr 2023

The daily dual that lost.

Cotadutide (MEDI0382)Development halted

AZ's once-daily GLP-1/glucagon dual — killed not by safety (none found) but by strategy: a daily shot couldn't compete with weeklies. The efficacy was real — roughly 3–5%+ weight loss at higher doses, better blood sugar, and liver-fat improvements. AZ pivoted to the weekly AZD9550 in the same class.

Maker: AstraZenecaReceptors: GLP-1 + glucagonForm: daily shot (was)
— program terminated

A good molecule beaten by a better dosing schedule.

13

Survodutide

Jun 2023

The liver-first dual agonist.

SurvodutideInvestigational

Another GLP-1 plus glucagon dual agonist — but with a distinctive angle: it's also being tested for liver fat disease (MASH), where the glucagon component directly attacks hepatic fat. Phase 3 SYNCHRONIZE program underway.

Maker: Boehringer Ingelheim / ZealandReceptors: GLP-1 + glucagonForm: weekly shot
12–15% weight loss in phase 2 (up to ~19% in completers)

Mid-range weight loss with a liver story the others don't have — though stomach side effects can run higher than some options.

14

Retatrutide

Jun 2023

The triple — highest trial numbers so far, still experimental.

RetatrutideInvestigational

A triple-action drug: GLP-1 + GIP + glucagon. In trials it produced the highest average weight loss seen so far — the full three-hormone stack doing what the map in chapter 2 predicted.

Maker: Eli LillyReceptors: GLP-1 + GIP + glucagonForm: weekly shot
24–28% weight loss in trials (48+ weeks, top doses)

Still experimental, not approved — and the stronger the stack, the more intense the early stomach issues and occasional faster heart rate. The one everyone is waiting on.

15

Efinopegdutide

Jun 2023

The liver specialist.

Efinopegdutide (MK-6024)Investigational

A GLP-1/glucagon dual that failed in obesity (J&J walked away) and was reborn as a liver drug: its obesity trials showed 8–12% weight loss over 26 weeks (dose-dependent) with manageable stomach effects; in Phase 2a, liver fat fell from 20% to under 5% — beating the GLP-1 comparator. FDA fast-tracked for MASH; Phase 3 planned 2027.

Maker: Merck / HanmiReceptors: GLP-1 + glucagonForm: weekly shot
— liver-fat focused (MASH)

The comeback story: wrong indication, right molecule.

16

Pemvidutide

Nov 2023

The muscle-sparer.

PemvidutideInvestigational

A balanced 1:1 GLP-1/glucagon dual whose signature is body composition: 78% of lost weight was fat, only 22% lean mass — best-in-class muscle preservation. Also in Phase 2b for MASH (IMPACT), with a Phase 3 PERFORMA planned.

Maker: AltimmuneReceptors: GLP-1 + glucagonForm: weekly shot
15.6% weight loss at 48 weeks (MOMENTUM Phase 2)

For anyone whose worry is "what kind of weight am I losing?" — this is the one to watch.

17

Amycretin

Mar 2024

Two hormones, one molecule, two formats.

Amycretin (NN9487)Investigational

Two hormones, one molecule, two formats. Amycretin fuses GLP-1 and amylin agonism into a single molecule — as a daily oral pill and a weekly injection. Early data stunned: the pill matched injected GLP-1s at 12 weeks; the shot rivaled retatrutide at 36. Phase 3 starting 2026.

Maker: Novo NordiskReceptors: GLP-1 + amylinForm: daily pill; weekly shot (both in trials)
13–24% oral 13.1% at 12 wks; injectable up to 24.3% at 36 wks (early trials)

If the numbers hold in Phase 3, this is the next CagriSema — no assembly required.

18

Mazdutide

Jun 2024

China's dual agonist — approved there, watched everywhere.

MazdutideApproved in China

A dual agonist hitting GLP-1 and glucagon — the glucagon arm burns extra energy and targets liver fat. Approved by China's NMPA for chronic weight management (first dual GCG/GLP-1 agonist marketed), based on the GLORY-1 phase 3 trial.

Maker: Innovent / Eli LillyReceptors: GLP-1 + glucagonForm: weekly shot
11–18% weight loss in Chinese trials (24–60 weeks)

Further along in approval outside the United States than most newer agents — well tolerated for many, and the one to watch for global expansion.

19

Cagrilintide

Dec 2024

The amylin outsider — modest alone, mighty in combination.

CagrilintideInvestigational

Cagrilintide copies amylin, the satiety hormone released alongside insulin after meals. It works in different brain regions than GLP-1 — which is why pairing them hits harder than either alone.

Maker: Novo NordiskReceptors: amylinForm: weekly shot
8–11% weight loss alone in trials

CagriSema — cagrilintide + semaglutide — has shown ~20–22% weight loss in studies (REDEFINE-1), more than semaglutide alone, with stomach side effects staying manageable for many. Some experiment with adding it to tirzepatide, though formal data for that pair is limited. An NDA has been filed.

20

Petrelintide

Mar 2025

Amylin, refined.

PetrelintideInvestigational

A long-acting amylin analog — cagrilintide's refined sibling — with a placebo-like tolerability profile even at top doses. Roche paid $1.65B upfront to co-develop it; Phase 3 planned for late 2026, plus a combo trial with Roche's CT-388.

Maker: Zealand / RocheReceptors: amylinForm: weekly shot
~11% weight loss at 42 weeks (ZUPREME-1 Phase 2)

Amylin's second act: similar satiety, far gentler stomach.

21

MariTide

Jun 2025

Once a month — and it blocks GIP.

MariTide (maridebart cafraglutide)Investigational

Once a month — and it blocks GIP instead of activating it. MariTide (maridebart cafraglutide) is a peptide-antibody conjugate: a GLP-1 agonist fused to a GIP-receptor-blocking antibody. Phase 3 MARITIME trials are enrolling.

Maker: AmgenReceptors: GLP-1 + GIP blockadeForm: monthly shot
12–16% weight loss at 52 weeks — up to ~20% in those who stayed on it (Phase 2, no plateau)

The contrarian bet: the field spent years turning GIP on — Amgen is turning it off.

22

Orforglipron

Apr 2026

The pill that plays like a shot.

Orforglipron (Foundayo)FDA-approved

The first oral small-molecule (non-peptide) GLP-1 to complete Phase 3 — a true daily pill with no food or water restrictions, unlike Rybelsus. ACHIEVE-1 also cut A1c by up to 1.6%. FDA-approved April 2026 for obesity and overweight (brand Foundayo).

Maker: Eli Lilly (Chugai-origin)Receptors: GLP-1Form: once-daily oral pill
12.4% weight loss at 17.2 mg / 72 weeks (ATTAIN-1, obesity)

The needle-free milestone the field waited twenty years for — GLP-1 as an ordinary pill.

23

Ribupatide

Jun 2026

China's dual — shot and pill.

RibupatideInvestigational

A GLP-1/GIP dual agonist being developed as both a weekly injection and a once-daily oral pill — studied in 2,500+ participants out to 52 weeks. The oral version posted ~12% weight loss at 26 weeks in Phase 2, with mostly mild stomach effects and lower vomiting rates than some peers. Hengrui has filed for NMPA approval for weight management; Kailera is running the global Phase 3 KaiNETIC program.

Maker: Hengrui / KaileraReceptors: GLP-1 + GIPForm: weekly shot; daily pill in trials
— late-stage; NDA filed in China

The dual-agonist race goes oral — and China is first in line.

Bioglutide

Phase 2

The quiet one — early, oral, multi-pathway.

Bioglutide (NA-931)Early research

Less widely discussed in major published trials. Described as an experimental multi-receptor agonist (reported as hitting GLP-1 plus additional pathways, with IGF-1 mentioned) — notably being developed as a once-daily oral tablet, which would be unusual in this class.

Maker: Biomed IndustriesReceptors: multi (early data)Form: oral (in development)
— public weight-loss numbers limited

Still in early research stages — detailed efficacy and side-effect data are thin compared with the others here. Interesting for the oral angle; unproven everywhere else.

Rhythms

Daily, weekly, or a pill — the routine matters as much as the molecule.

📅Daily: liraglutide, lixisenatide (shots); beinaglutide (mealtime shots); orforglipron, oral semaglutide/Rybelsus (pills)
🗓️Weekly: exenatide (Bydureon), dulaglutide, semaglutide, tirzepatide, cagrilintide, ribupatide, retatrutide, mazdutide, survodutide, pemvidutide, petrelintide — the modern standard
🗓️Monthly: maritide — dosed monthly or even less often
Why weekly won: same hormone logic, one-seventh the injections. Adherence climbs when the routine fades into the background — a real-world effect no trial fully captures.

Side effects

The stomach leads everywhere. The pattern is the same; the intensity isn't.

For almost all nine of the original set — semaglutide, liraglutide, dulaglutide, tirzepatide, cagrilintide, mazdutide, survodutide, retatrutide and bioglutide — side effects center on the stomach: nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, constipation, and reduced appetite are the most common. They often start strong when the dose is raised, then calm down — which is why every one of these uses slow dose escalation. Clinical trial

🌶️Stronger stacks, stronger start: retatrutide and higher-dose dual agonists can cause more intense stomach issues and sometimes a faster heart rate.
➕Combos stay manageable: cagrilintide combinations keep side effects similar to semaglutide alone for many people.
🪶Gentler outlier: petrelintide's stomach effects ran close to placebo levels in Phase 2 — the mildest profile in the class so far.
Rarer but serious: pancreas inflammation (pancreatitis), gallbladder problems — and an animal-study thyroid tumor signal that puts a boxed warning on the GLP-1 labels (personal/family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma or MEN 2 means don't use). Regular check-ups catch problems early.

Choosing

Goals, tolerance, cost, availability — in that order, with a doctor.

🏆Strongest possible loss, tolerates intensity: retatrutide when available, or tirzepatide today.
❤️Well-tested weekly with heart data: semaglutide — slightly less loss than tirzepatide, deeper evidence.
🩸Mainly blood sugar, modest loss: dulaglutide — the diabetes-first pick.
💉Comfortable with daily pens: liraglutide — older, sometimes better covered.
🧪Newest duals (mazdutide, survodutide): mid-range loss, less long-term safety data, limited availability.
💊Needle-free and approved today: orforglipron — the once-daily GLP-1 pill with no fasting rules.
📜Legacy options: exenatide and lixisenatide still exist but are rarely first-choice now; albiglutide was withdrawn for commercial reasons, not safety.
📅Once-monthly, strongest pipeline: MariTide — monthly dosing with up to ~20% loss in those who stayed on it, still investigational.
🪶Mildest stomach profile: petrelintide — gentler GI effects than the GLP-1s, moderate ~11% loss, still in trials.
On combinations: duals and triples already stack pathways in one shot, so separate add-ons are uncommon — the live frontier is amylin + GLP-1 pairing (CagriSema-style): more fullness than either alone, with a bit more stomach risk. The honest trade: newer multi-agonists bring bigger losses and better blood sugar — but higher cost, stronger early side effects, and thinner long-term records. Older singles bring track records and coverage — but smaller numbers. A doctor matches the drug to the main problem: blood sugar, weight, or both.

Understanding GLP Terminology

Fourteen terms, each with its scientific meaning and a plain-English mirror. Tap each term.

Scientific MeaningGlucagon-like peptide-1 is an incretin hormone released by L-cells of the small intestine when food arrives. It boosts insulin secretion from pancreatic beta cells only when blood sugar is elevated, suppresses glucagon, slows gastric emptying, and signals appetite centers in the brain to reduce food intake. Natural GLP-1 is destroyed within minutes by the enzyme DPP-4, so every drug version is engineered to resist breakdown and last far longer. It is the backbone target of all the approved medicines in this masterclass.
Plain EnglishGLP-1 is a gut hormone your body releases when you eat — it tells the pancreas to release insulin (only if sugar is high), tells the stomach to empty slower, and tells the brain you're getting full. The natural version vanishes in minutes, so drugmakers built tougher, longer-lasting copies. Every approved drug in this guide is built on this one hormone signal.
Scientific MeaningGlucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide is the second major incretin hormone, secreted by K-cells of the upper intestine after meals. Like GLP-1, it amplifies insulin release in a glucose-dependent manner, and it also acts on fat tissue and the brain. Tirzepatide, ribupatide and others activate the GIP receptor alongside GLP-1 for additive weight loss. MariTide takes the opposite approach, blocking the GIP receptor with an antibody while stimulating GLP-1.
Plain EnglishGIP is GLP-1's sibling hormone — also released after eating, also boosting insulin only when sugar is high. Most newer drugs turn its receptor ON together with GLP-1 for extra weight loss. MariTide is the rebel: it turns the GIP receptor OFF while keeping GLP-1 on, and still posted ~20% weight loss.
Scientific MeaningGlucagon is a pancreatic alpha-cell hormone that raises blood sugar by instructing the liver to release stored glucose. Counterintuitively, activating its receptor helps treat obesity: glucagon signaling increases energy expenditure and drives fat breakdown in the liver. Mazdutide, survodutide, retatrutide, pemvidutide and efinopegdutide all pair glucagon-receptor activation with GLP-1 to stack appetite suppression on top of higher calorie burn. The balance matters — too much glucagon action would raise blood sugar, so these are carefully tuned co-agonists.
Plain EnglishGlucagon is usually the hormone that raises blood sugar — the opposite of insulin. But flipping its receptor on also makes the body burn more energy and melt liver fat. That's why half the new drugs pair it with GLP-1: one quiets hunger, the other turns up the metabolic furnace. Tuned carefully, you get the burn without the blood-sugar spike.
Scientific MeaningAmylin is a peptide hormone co-secreted with insulin by pancreatic beta cells in response to meals. It promotes satiety, slows gastric emptying, and suppresses post-meal glucagon — a complementary brake on eating alongside GLP-1. Cagrilintide and petrelintide are long-acting amylin analogues; amycretin fuses GLP-1 and amylin activity into a single molecule. The CagriSema combination (cagrilintide + semaglutide) reached ~20–22% weight loss in Phase 3, more than either component alone.
Plain EnglishAmylin is a second fullness hormone, released with insulin every time you eat — it slows the stomach and tells the brain 'that's enough.' Cagrilintide and petrelintide are long-lasting copies of it. Pair one with semaglutide (that's CagriSema) and you get ~20%+ weight loss — the satiety double-act behind the biggest combo numbers here.
Scientific MeaningIncretins are gut hormones — principally GLP-1 and GIP — secreted after eating that amplify insulin release in a strictly glucose-dependent way, a phenomenon called the incretin effect. Because the insulin boost fades as blood sugar normalizes, incretin-based drugs carry a low intrinsic risk of hypoglycemia compared with older diabetes drugs. The entire medication class in this masterclass is built on mimicking, extending, or stacking incretin signals. Understanding incretins is understanding why these drugs lower both appetite and blood sugar together.
Plain EnglishIncretins are the gut's 'food is here' messengers — mainly GLP-1 and GIP — that tell the pancreas to release insulin, but only while sugar is actually high. That glucose-dependence is the safety genius: the effect switches itself off as sugar normalizes, so lows are rare. This whole drug family is just increasingly clever ways of copying and stacking those messengers.
Scientific MeaningA receptor agonist is a molecule that binds to a cellular receptor and activates it, reproducing the natural signal. Nearly every drug in this guide is a GLP-1 receptor agonist — a engineered key that turns the GLP-1 lock on. Agonists are the opposite of antagonists, which occupy the receptor but block activation. Small-molecule agonists like orforglipron achieve the same receptor activation with compact synthetic chemistry instead of a peptide chain.
Plain EnglishAn agonist is a molecule that fits a cell's receptor like a key and turns it ON — copying the body's own signal. Almost everything in this guide is a GLP-1 receptor agonist: a synthetic key for the GLP-1 lock. The opposite, an antagonist, sits in the lock and jams it — which is what MariTide does to the GIP receptor.
Scientific MeaningA dual agonist is a single drug molecule engineered to activate two different receptors. Tirzepatide (GLP-1 + GIP), mazdutide and survodutide (GLP-1 + glucagon), and cagrilintide-based combos each stack two hormonal pathways in one medicine. The rationale is synergy: appetite suppression from one pathway plus energy expenditure or extra satiety from the second. Dual agonists produced the step-change from ~15% to ~20%+ weight loss in trials.
Plain EnglishA dual agonist is one drug that flips two hormone switches at once — tirzepatide does GLP-1 plus GIP; mazdutide does GLP-1 plus glucagon. Two pathways, one shot. That stacking is what jumped trial results from the ~15% era into the ~20% era.
Scientific MeaningA triple agonist activates three receptors simultaneously — currently exemplified by retatrutide (GLP-1 + GIP + glucagon), the only triple agonist in late-stage trials. It combines appetite suppression, incretin-driven insulin support, and glucagon-mediated energy expenditure in one weekly injection. In Phase 2 it posted the highest weight-loss numbers in the class at ~24% over 48 weeks. Triple agonism is the current frontier of the stacking strategy.
Plain EnglishA triple agonist flips three switches at once — retatrutide is the only one this far along: GLP-1 plus GIP plus glucagon in a single weekly shot. Hunger down, insulin supported, calorie burn up. Its ~24% trial weight loss is the number everything else is now chasing.
Scientific MeaningTitration is the gradual escalation of dose — starting low and stepping upward over weeks — used to let the body adapt. Every drug in this class is titrated because jumping straight to the full dose provokes intense nausea, vomiting, and other gastrointestinal effects. The slow climb is why side effects 'start strong when the dose is raised, then calm down.' Titration schedules are trial-design facts, not recommendations, and appear on every approved label.
Plain EnglishTitration means starting on a tiny dose and stair-stepping up over weeks so your gut can adjust. Skip the stairs and go straight to the top dose, and the nausea will flatten you — that's why every one of these drugs climbs slowly. It's the reason side effects flare with each increase, then settle.
Scientific MeaningHalf-life is the time required for half of a drug to be cleared from the body, and it dictates dosing frequency. The class history is a half-life story: twice-daily exenatide gave way to daily liraglutide, then weekly semaglutide, dulaglutide and tirzepatide, and now monthly MariTide in trials. Longer half-lives come from engineering — fatty-acid chains, antibody fusions, or albumin binding that slow clearance. Convenience improves with half-life, but so does commitment: a weekly or monthly drug lingers if side effects appear.
Plain EnglishHalf-life is how long half the drug sticks around in your body — and it decides how often you dose. The whole class marched from twice-daily (exenatide) to daily to weekly to monthly (MariTide) purely by engineering longer half-lives. More convenient, but also more commitment: a monthly shot can't be 'un-taken' if you feel rough.
Scientific MeaningHbA1c (glycated hemoglobin) measures average blood glucose over roughly three months and is the standard yardstick for diabetes control. Many of these drugs were first approved for diabetes on the strength of HbA1c reductions — oral semaglutide cut it by 1.4% in PIONEER-1, for example. Weight-loss and diabetes indications share the same molecules but are judged by different primary endpoints. A lower HbA1c means steadier blood sugar and lower complication risk.
Plain EnglishHbA1c is the three-month average blood sugar test — the number diabetes care revolves around. Plenty of these drugs earned their first approvals by moving it: Rybelsus cut it 1.4% in its key trial. Same molecules as the weight-loss versions, judged by a different scoreboard.
Scientific MeaningA placebo is an inactive control treatment used in randomized trials so the drug's true effect can be isolated from expectation, behavior change, and time. Every percentage in this masterclass — weight loss, A1c reduction, heart-event cuts — is measured against placebo, with diet and exercise advice given to both groups. Large placebo responses are common in obesity trials, which is why the drug-minus-placebo gap matters more than the raw number. 'Beats placebo' is the minimum bar; the interesting question is by how much.
Plain EnglishA placebo is the dummy pill or shot in a trial — the control group that lets scientists separate the drug's real effect from hope, diet advice, and time. Every number in this guide is drug-versus-placebo, and both sides got the same diet-and-exercise coaching. The gap between the two lines is the number that actually matters.
Scientific MeaningUnlike peptide GLP-1 drugs — chains of amino acids that must be injected or use absorption enhancers — orforglipron is a small-molecule drug: a compact synthetic chemical that activates the GLP-1 receptor, survives the digestive tract, and is taken as an ordinary once-daily pill with no food or water restrictions. It is the first of its kind to complete Phase 3 trials and reach FDA approval.
Plain EnglishEvery GLP-1 drug until now was a fragile protein that the stomach would destroy — hence shots, or a special pill with strict fasting rules. Orforglipron is different chemistry entirely: a tough little molecule that flips the same GLP-1 switch but survives as a normal daily pill. Same receptor, completely different packaging.
Scientific MeaningMost drugs in this class activate (agonize) the GIP receptor. MariTide does the opposite on that target: an antibody component blocks the GIP receptor while a fused peptide stimulates GLP-1. The working theory is that chronic GIP blockade dampens fat-storage signaling while GLP-1 handles appetite — a contrarian pairing that produced ~20% weight loss at 52 weeks in Phase 2 with monthly dosing.
Plain EnglishThe whole field spent years learning how to turn the GIP switch ON harder. MariTide turns it OFF — while turning GLP-1 on. It's the odd one out by design: block one hunger pathway's helper, stimulate the other, and dose just once a month. Early results say the contrarian bet might pay off.

Takeaways — and the limits

Thirty chapters, distilled.

🧬One playbook, five levers. GLP-1 is the foundation; GIP, glucagon, amylin, and IGF-1 are the stacks — each generation has meant bigger average losses.
📊The range is real. ~3–6% (dulaglutide) to ~24–28% (retatrutide) across trials — shapes, not champions.
➕Combos are the frontier. CagriSema (~20–22%) shows what pairing pathways can do.
🤢The gut leads. Nausea and GI issues everywhere; slow escalation is non-negotiable.
🌍Status is geography. Four FDA-approved, one China-approved, four investigational or early.
🏛️Twenty years of stacking. Twice-daily exenatide (2005) → weekly everything → a GLP-1 pill (2026) and monthly shots in trials. The arc bends toward more pathways, longer intervals, easier formats.
🔁Stopping means regain. Plan for the long term, with food, movement, and check-ups.

Limits of this page

  • Trial numbers aren't comparable. Different populations, durations, and designs — treat every percentage as approximate.
  • Many are investigational or limited. Cagrilintide, ribupatide, survodutide, retatrutide, maritide, amycretin, petrelintide, pemvidutide, efinopegdutide, and bioglutide lack approvals in most countries; mazdutide and beinaglutide are China-only; albiglutide was withdrawn; cotadutide's development was halted.
  • Real-world results run lower than trials — adherence and lifestyle matter enormously.
  • All work best with healthy eating and movement — none is a standalone fix.
  • Not medical advice. Choosing among these depends on goals, side-effect tolerance, cost, availability, and health conditions — a doctor's job, not a webpage's.
